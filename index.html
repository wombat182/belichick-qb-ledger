Belichick never bought a quarterback

The charge is that he can't evaluate the position. Priced properly — in draft capital spent against NFL starts produced — he is the most efficient quarterback drafter of his generation, and it isn't close. The real criticism is a different one, and it survives.

The unit

A draft pick is a price

A draft is a trade: you give up a pick, you get a player. So every quarterback a coach drafts has a sticker price, and the Jimmy Johnson chart is the price list the league has used since 1991.

It was built for Dallas by executive Mike McCoy, reverse-engineered from what teams actually paid each other for picks. The first pick costs 3,000 points. The 32nd costs 590. The 199th costs 11.8.

That last number is the whole argument in one line. Tom Brady cost eleven point eight points — four tenths of one percent of the first overall pick, and about the going rate for a seventh-rounder who never plays a down.

The Brady test

Delete every coach's best quarterback

The obvious objection: Belichick's record is one player. True — but so is everyone's. Every coach here has exactly one quarterback holding up his entire case, and taking that player away is the fair test applied evenly.

Coach, minus his best QBQBs leftNet capital StartsPro BowlsNet / start
 Mike Tomlin is absent because he has no quarterback to remove: eight drafted in twenty drafts, zero Pro Bowls, zero 30-start seasons of a career between them.

Without Brady, Belichick's remaining fourteen quarterbacks started 300 NFL games — more than Andy Reid's nine non-Mahomes quarterbacks managed (259), on less than half the capital after trades. Two of them made Pro Bowls: Matt Cassel in 2010 and Mac Jones in 2021. Strip Mahomes from Reid, Purdy from Shanahan, Wilson from Carroll, and what's left produces nothing at all.

Every coach in this sample is a one-quarterback coach. The difference is what the one quarterback cost. Brady cost 11.8 points. Mahomes cost 1,596. Wilson cost 215. Lamar Jackson cost 590.

The free one

Brian Hoyer went undrafted in 2009 and New England signed him. He started 41 NFL games across fifteen seasons and eight franchises — more than Bo Nix, who cost Sean Payton the 12th overall pick and 1,200 points. Hoyer cost nothing and isn't in any table here, which is itself the point: the pipeline was never about the picks.

The base rate

Round one works, and it is a terrible deal

Pool all 53 quarterbacks and sort them by where they were taken. Both halves of the answer are uncomfortable.

First-rounders hit far more often — five of eight made a Pro Bowl, against two of nineteen in rounds five through seven. Anyone claiming the draft is pure noise is wrong. But the first-rounders consumed 11,358 points to produce 719 starts, and the picks after #224 consumed 7 points to produce 128. Scouting works. Paying for it is the losing half of the trade, and Belichick is the only coach here who consistently refused to.

The charge that survives

He was cheap because he had Brady, and then he wasn't

Two honest objections remain, and the numbers support both.

One: the efficiency is partly circumstance. For nineteen years Belichick didn't need a quarterback, so he bought lottery tickets instead of groceries. That is a cheap portfolio by necessity, not only by insight. A coach who must start a rookie cannot take a flier at 219 and wait.

Two: the one time he had to solve it, he didn't. Mac Jones at pick 15 cost 1,050 points — 49% of everything Belichick ever spent on the position across 29 drafts — and produced 57 starts and one replacement Pro Bowl before being traded for a sixth-rounder. After that came Bailey Zappe at 137. New England went 4–13 in his final season, and the quarterback room was the reason. He drafted no quarterback at all in 2023.

So the defensible version is narrow and specific: after Brady, Belichick did not solve the position, and his one real swing missed. That is true. It is also true of almost everyone — Shanahan's swing missed by 2,572 points and Tomlin has missed eight times in a row.

"Bad at evaluating quarterbacks" describes a man who spends heavily and picks wrong. Belichick spent less on the position than any coach of his era, recouped half of it in trades, and found the best quarterback who ever played in the sixth round. Whatever went wrong in New England after 2019, that isn't it.

The verdict

Method. Draft capital priced on the original Jimmy Johnson chart (picks 1–224; picks past 224 valued at 1 point). A pick's cost is its slot value, except where the team traded up specifically to take that quarterback, in which case the cost is the full value of every pick sent — this applies to Mahomes (1,596), Trey Lance (2,572) and C.J. Beathard (79.8). Capital recouped is the slot value of picks received when the drafting coach later traded that quarterback; Cassel's return is credited in full although Mike Vrabel was in the package, and Kevin Kolb's although Dominique Rodgers-Cromartie was. Pro Bowls and starts are career-wide, all teams. 2026 rookies (Nussmeier, Allar, Mendoza) and 2025 picks who have not yet played are excluded from rate statistics but carried in capital totals. Cleveland 1991–92 picks are included though Ernie Accorsi was still general manager; removing Keithen McCant changes nothing.

Known limits. The Johnson chart overprices the top of the board — it was fitted to what teams paid, not to what picks produced — so it flatters any strategy that avoids round one, including this one. Career starts reward longevity and durability as much as quality, and Pro Bowls are a popularity vote. Foles' pick arrived bundled with DeMeco Ryans and can't be cleanly attributed. Figures are through the end of the 2025 season.
